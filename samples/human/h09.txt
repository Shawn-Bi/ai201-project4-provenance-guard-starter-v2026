What is the project you are most proud of, and why?
S: Two years ago, someone close to me went through a very difficult period with their mental health. After trying repeatedly to seek psychological support, they were continually told to keep waiting, with no estimated timeline. I also heard that some people booked appointments with mental health professionals even though they did not genuinely need psychological support. I realized that people in a mental health crisis might have to endure an unknown wait on their own for a very long time, and some might not make it through that waiting period. That became the starting point for AegisBot.
T: I wanted to build an AI-assisted mental health crisis triage prototype to help organizations prioritize their limited human resources for people at the highest risk. But AI must never make the final determination of whether someone is in danger.
A: My initial approach was to turn help requests into assessable inputs through a structured questionnaire. A rules engine and an ML model would evaluate risk independently, and the higher risk assessment would determine priority—better to review more cases than miss a high-risk case. A nonnegotiable rule was that the system would provide explainable reasons only, with final decisions remaining with humans. About 120 test cases defined the required behavior in scenarios where assessments disagreed.
R: The prototype has a demo and code, but it has not been clinically validated. I never describe it as ready for real-world use and clearly state that it is for demonstration only.
